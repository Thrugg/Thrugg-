Why not just "6 to 12 months"?

That rule assumes nothing replaces your salary when you lose your job. In most of Europe, unemployment insurance replaces a large share of it for months, sometimes years. An emergency fund therefore only has to cover what the insurance does not: the days before benefits start, the gap between the benefit and your essential spending, the months after benefits run out, and the sanction if you resign.

What the model does

  1. It estimates your net benefit from each country's 2026 formula, caps, step-downs and duration. Germany uses a full 2026 wage-tax calculation (§ 153 SGB III); other countries apply a stated deduction and tax rate that you can change.
  2. It simulates 365–730 days. Each day you spend your essential budget (minus household income that continues). Benefits accrue after the waiting days and arrive after the first-payment delay.
  3. The highest cumulative drawdown within a horizon is the cash you would have needed. Scenario A is an involuntary job loss. Scenario B is a resignation, with that country's sanction (a delay, a cut, or no benefit at all).

The five levels

Basic = surprise bill + spending during (waiting days + first-payment delay) Good = surprise bill + peak drawdown, job loss, typical search (6 mo) Very good = surprise bill + peak drawdown, job loss, long search (12 mo) Excellent = surprise bill + peak drawdown, resignation, long search (12 mo) Ceiling = Excellent + a second surprise bill Each level is at least 0.5 month of essentials above the one before it. This leaves margin for friction the rules do not capture.

The score

The score runs from 0 to 100 and uses the same band cut-offs as Apple's Sleep Score since watchOS 26.2: 0–40, 41–60, 61–80, 81–95, 96–100. Reaching Basic gives 41, Good 61, Very good 81, Excellent 96 and the Ceiling 100. Between two levels the score moves in a straight line. Above the Ceiling it stays at 100, and the page shows the idle cash with the return it gives up at your chosen rate. That figure is an illustration, not a forecast.

What is not modelled

  • Long illness, disability, self-employment and fixed-term contract ends. Sick pay and disability cover are better handled with insurance than with cash.
  • Severance, notice periods and garden leave. They usually shorten the gap, so leaving them out makes the result more conservative. Italy's TFR and Austria's Abfertigung are mentioned in the rules.
  • Means-tested support after benefits end, such as Grundsicherung, bijstand or kontanthjælp. Your savings would have to be used up first, so the model counts it as zero. Austria's Notstandshilfe is included because it is not means-tested on partner income.
  • Exact tax. Germany and Austria pay tax-free benefits, and the other countries use the editable rate. Germany's Progressionsvorbehalt can still create a tax bill the following spring.
  • Special cases: the extensions for older workers apart from those listed, Belgian 55+ exemptions, frontier workers, and the Swiss extra allowances before retirement.

Moving countries

Within the EU/EEA and Switzerland, insurance periods from your previous country generally count towards eligibility once you have started working in the new one (Regulation (EC) 883/2004, form PD U1). The benefit is then based on your new local pay. In Denmark it depends on a-kasse membership, so join on day one. The Compare tab converts your current profile at ECB rates. Open a country in My score and enter local pay and spending for a sharper answer. Results in months of spending hold up better than the amounts.

Sources and limits

Each country's parameters and links are listed under Rules & sources, as of 26 September 2026. The engine is tested against the published maximums (for example CH CHF 8,645, FR 300.21 €/day, IT 1,584.70 €, NL ≈ 5,055 €). For Germany it is tested against the Federal Employment Agency's own worked example, which it matches to within a few cents a day. An Excel twin with the same rules gives identical results.
